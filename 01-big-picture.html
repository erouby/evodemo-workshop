1  The big picture

NoteWhat you’ll be able to do

Explain, in plain words, what the EvoDemo-Hyper MPM adds to an ordinary matrix population model, and name the four vital rates and the genetic quantities the model tracks.

1.1 Two ways of thinking about change

When a population meets a changing environment, two research communities reach for different tools.

Demographers describe a population as numbers of individuals in stages — juveniles, adults — and project them forward with a matrix population model (MPM). This captures the life cycle beautifully but, in its classic form, has no genetics: it cannot let a trait evolve.

Evolutionary quantitative geneticists describe a trait — its mean, its heritable (additive genetic) variance — and predict its response to selection with the breeder’s equation. This captures evolution but tends to flatten the life cycle into a single “generation”.

These two approaches have often led to different conclusions about the pace and consequences of adaptation because they emphasise different biological processes (Van de Walle et al. 2025). The EvoDemo-Hyper MPM brings these perspectives together by combining a full demographic life cycle with the dynamics of a heritable trait within a single matrix framework that remains computationally efficient and transparent to analyse.

TipKey idea

The EvoDemo-Hyper MPM is an ordinary matrix model with two extra dimensions bolted on: besides stage, every individual also carries a breeding value (its heritable genetic contribution) and a phenotype. Selection reshapes the trait distribution; inheritance passes it to offspring.

1.2 The life cycle

Everything in this workshop rests on one simple, two-stage life cycle: juveniles (J) and adults (A), censused just before breeding, with annual time steps.

flowchart LR
  J(("Juvenile<br/>J")) -->|"S_J · γ — mature"| A(("Adult<br/>A"))
  J -->|"S_J · (1−γ) — stay J"| J
  A -->|"S_A — survive"| A
  A -->|"F — reproduce"| J

Four vital rates govern the transitions:

  • \(S_J\) — juvenile survival,
  • \(S_A\) — adult survival,
  • \(\gamma\) — the probability a surviving juvenile matures into an adult,
  • \(F\) — fertility, the per-capita number of juveniles an adult contributes next year (\(F = f \times S_J\)).

Stacked into a projection matrix, the demography is just a \(2\times2\) Leslie matrix:

\[ \mathbf{A} = \begin{pmatrix} S_J(1-\gamma) & F \\[2pt] S_J\,\gamma & S_A \end{pmatrix}. \]

That is the entire demographic skeleton. The five species you will meet later differ only in the numbers that fill these four slots.

1.3 Adding genetics

Now give every individual a quantitative trait \(z\) that influences one of the vital rates. Following the classic decomposition, the phenotype is a population mean plus a heritable breeding value and an environmental deviation:

\[ z = \bar{z} + a + e, \qquad V_P = V_A + V_E, \qquad h^2 = \frac{V_A}{V_P}. \]

Here \(V_P\) is the phenotypic variance, \(V_A\) the additive genetic variance, and \(h^2\) the heritability — the fraction of trait variation that is passed on. Selection acts on \(z\); only the breeding value \(a\) is inherited.

The model discretises both the breeding value and the phenotype into a grid of classes (centred on zero, spanning \(\pm\) a range you choose). So instead of tracking “how many juveniles and adults”, it tracks how many individuals of each (stage, breeding value, phenotype) combination — a three-dimensional hyperstate. Offspring draw their breeding value from their parents via the Fisher infinitesimal model, which is how evolution enters.

You do not need the matrix algebra to use the model — we open that engine in Chapter 5. For now, hold on to this:

TipThe model in one sentence

Project individuals through a structured life cycle while tracking the distribution of breeding values and phenotypes, allowing selection, inheritance, and demography to operate simultaneously within a single matrix framework.

ImportantYour turn — make a prediction

A fast-living grass and a slow-living albatross face the same selection pressure on the same heritable trait. Which one do you expect to adapt faster per year? Write your guess down — we will test it in Chapter 4, and the answer is more interesting than it looks.
